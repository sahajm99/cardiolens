Is it just age?

Ask this file almost any question and part of the answer is age. Among adults aged 18 to 24, … report heart disease. Among adults aged 80 and older it is …, about … as high.

That gradient is the spine of everything below it. Any two groups that differ in age inherit part of it before they differ in anything else.

Age is not the only line through this sample. Smokers report heart disease at … against … of non-smokers, a crude gap of …. Adults who report no physical activity sit at … against … of physically active adults, a crude gap of …. Side by side, the two look like the same finding.

A crude gap compares two groups that differ in more than the factor it names, and the obvious suspect is age. So hold age fixed: the chart below splits one factor at a time into age bands and draws both curves inside every band.

Change the factor with the picker. Some pairs of curves stay apart in every band; others close up once age is held fixed, and the claim above the chart says which.

What survives

Holding age fixed one factor at a time only goes so far, because the factors also travel with each other. A logistic model can hold all of them fixed at once and report what each one is still associated with.

This page fits two. The primary model carries age band, sex, BMI class, smoking, heavy drinking, diabetes, stroke, kidney disease, asthma and skin cancer. The second adds physical activity, difficulty walking and self-rated health.

Those last three sit in their own panel because they are plausibly consequences of heart disease rather than causes of it. A person who has had a heart attack may well report difficulty walking and fair health because of it. Put them in the same column as smoking and they absorb the effect of everything upstream of them.

The crude gap for inactivity (…) becomes an adjusted odds ratio of … for inactive against active adults once age and self-rated health are held fixed: the gap is gone. Smoking's does not go (…), and neither does a prior stroke (…), kidney disease (…) or diabetes (…).

One row reads the wrong way round. Heavy drinking lands below 1, which would say heavy drinkers report less heart disease. That is the sick-quitter pattern familiar in cross-sectional surveys: people who stop drinking after a diagnosis are counted with the non-drinkers, which loads the comparison group with the very people the question is about. It is not evidence that drinking protects anyone.

The same coefficients can be run forwards. Pick a set of answers and the panel evaluates the primary model for that profile, beside the survey average and the average for the same age band.

Interactions

Factors do not arrive one at a time. They land on the same people, and the combinations are where the numbers get large.

The grid below crosses BMI class with usual hours of sleep. Its loudest cells are not only the short sleepers: long sleep sits high too, so the pattern runs along both ends of the sleep range. Long sleep is itself a common marker of other illness, which is one reason not to read this grid as a set of instructions.

The buttons narrow the grid to adults reporting a given number of poor mental-health days in the last month. The cells thin out quickly, and the intervals in the table widen with them.

Sleep on its own next, inside three broad age groups, so the shape can be read without the whole age gradient underneath it.

BMI carries more than one outcome with it. The chart below draws heart disease and stroke against the same five BMI classes, for one age group at a time.

Gaps this file cannot explain

This file records each respondent's race and ethnicity. It does not record income, education, insurance or where anybody lives. Those are the variables that usually carry most of a health gap between groups, and not one of them is here.

So this section describes and does not explain. No model on this page uses race as a covariate either: a coefficient fitted without those four variables invites exactly the causal reading the data cannot support.

Standardising for age is the usual first move, and on this file it does not close the gap. It moves individual groups, and not all in the same direction: Hispanic and Asian rates rise, because those groups are younger than the sample as a whole and a common age mix ages them. The distance from the highest group to the lowest stays about where it was, with … adults highest at … once every group sits on one age mix.

A prior stroke dominates every group it appears in, and the difference it makes is far larger than the differences between groups.

What people say about their own health differs by group as well, and self-rated health carried the largest odds ratios of any term in the model above apart from age itself.

The lifestyle measures this file does carry are far flatter between groups than heart disease is, which is the point of the section: what would account for the gap is not in the file.

What this data cannot tell you

Each of these applies to every number above it.

  • Everything is self-reported. The outcome is an answer to whether a doctor has ever said the respondent had coronary heart disease or a heart attack, so it measures a diagnosis a person remembers and is willing to repeat down the phone. Height and weight are self-reported too, and the BMI classes are built from them.
  • The file is a third party's extract. The 2020 survey reached … respondents. This file carries … rows. The … respondents in between were removed by the Kaggle author in a cleaning step that is not documented and cannot be reconstructed here, and whatever selection that introduced travels into every number on this page.
  • No survey weights and no clustering. BRFSS is a complex sample with weights and strata; the extract carries neither. Every percentage here is unweighted sample prevalence, so it describes the people in this file rather than US adults, and every interval is narrower than the truth.
  • It is a cross-section. Exposure and outcome were recorded in the same interview, so nothing here establishes direction. Several of the associations almost certainly run backwards: difficulty walking and fair self-rated health are at least as likely to follow heart disease as to precede it, which is why they are shown in their own panel.
  • … rows are exact duplicates, and they are kept. There is no respondent identifier, and with this few coarse columns two different people can answer identically. Dropping … of the sample on that suspicion would be an edit to the data, not a cleaning step.
  • No geography. The extract has no State column, so there are no maps here and no regional comparison.
  • The positive rate is …, and it needs no correction. No classifier is fitted anywhere in this project, so there is nothing to rebalance or resample. The rate matters in one way only: it sets how wide a subgroup's interval is, which is why thin cells are flagged and the thinnest are not drawn at all.
  • An odds ratio is not a prevalence ratio. The models report odds. At a prevalence this low the two numbers are close, but they are not the same number, and a large odds ratio on a rare outcome is a smaller difference in people than it looks.
  • Suppression is applied, not hidden. A cell built on too few adults carries no estimate at all, and a thin cell is drawn flagged rather than quietly. Every figure carries its own table, so the count behind each claim is one click away.
